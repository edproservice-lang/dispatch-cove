## The short answer

Independent truck dispatchers typically charge between 5% and 10% of gross freight revenue for percentage-based agreements, or a flat fee ranging from $150 to $300 per week per truck. The exact cost depends on your fleet size, the specific lanes you run, and whether the dispatcher handles billing, broker check-ins, and paperwork.

## Understanding Independent Dispatcher Pay

When you hire an independent dispatcher, you are bringing on an outside partner to secure freight, negotiate rates, and manage load paperwork. Unlike company dispatchers who are paid a salary by a motor carrier, independent dispatchers operate as independent businesses or agencies. They build their pricing models around the time, effort, and administrative load required to keep your truck moving profitable miles. Knowing the typical market rates helps owner-operators budget effectively and choose a pricing structure that matches their cash flow.

For a broader view of how these independent services differ from freight brokerages, review the details on our [Truck Dispatcher vs Freight Broker: What Is the Difference?](/getting-started/truck-dispatcher-vs-freight-broker/) page. Understanding this legal and operational boundary ensures you set clear expectations with your service provider right from the start.

## Common Dispatch Fee Structures

Dispatchers generally structure their pricing in a few standard ways. Choosing the right model depends on your weekly mileage, how many loads you book, and your preference for predictable expenses versus performance-based costs.

### Percentage of Gross Revenue

The most common pricing method is a percentage split based on the gross revenue of the loads booked. Independent dispatchers usually charge between 5% and 10% of the linehaul or gross load pay. If a dispatcher books $10,000 in freight for your flatbed or dry van in a given week at an 8% rate, your fee is $800. This model aligns the dispatcher's incentives with your own: higher-paying loads mean higher earnings for both parties. If your truck sits for maintenance or encounters slow market conditions, the dispatcher makes nothing for that period, which shares some of the operational risk.

### Flat Weekly Fees

Some dispatchers charge a flat weekly rate per truck, typically falling between $150 and $300. This structure works well for busy owner-operators who run consistent, high-volume lanes and want to cap their dispatch expenses. Whether the dispatcher books five short regional runs or two long-haul loads that gross a high total revenue, the weekly fee stays the same. This can save money during high-grossing weeks, but it places the full cost burden on the carrier during slow weeks when revenue drops.

### Per-Load Flat Rates

Less common, but still used by some independent agents, is a flat fee per load booked—often ranging from $25 to $50 per load. This model appeals to owner-operators who only need occasional dispatching assistance or spot coverage when their primary dispatcher is away. It keeps expenses strictly tied to volume, though it may lead dispatchers to prioritize volume over higher rate-per-mile negotiations if they are chasing quick per-load fees.

## What Factors Influence Dispatcher Rates?

Dispatcher pricing is not entirely uniform. Several operational and administrative variables can push rates toward the higher or lower end of the industry spectrum.

### Fleet Size and Equipment Type

Single-truck owner-operators often pay slightly higher percentage rates—closer to 8% or 10%—because managing one truck takes nearly as much initial setup time as managing a small multi-truck fleet. Conversely, small fleets with three or more trucks can often negotiate volume discounts down to 5% or 6%. Additionally, specialized equipment like heavy haul, step decks, or reefers requires more complex route planning, broker communication, and tarping coordination, which can command higher fees.

### Scope of Administrative Services

Some dispatchers strictly book loads and handle initial broker setup packets. Others provide full back-office support, including setting up credit checks, submitting paperwork to your factoring company, managing carrier compliance, and handling customer service check-ins. If your dispatcher takes on extra administrative work, expect the fee to reflect that added value. Before signing an agreement, you can use a [Dispatch fee calculator](/tools/dispatch-fee/) to test different percentage and flat-fee scenarios against your projected weekly gross to see how various pricing models affect your bottom line.

### Load Board and Tool Costs

Dispatchers typically use professional load boards like DAT or Truckstop to find available freight. While most established dispatchers pay for their own subscriptions, some newer or independent contractors may factor tool access into their overall pricing or require carriers to provide secondary user logins. To understand how these platforms operate and how dispatchers evaluate credit scores and load rates, read more about [DAT vs Truckstop for a New Dispatcher](/load-boards/dat-vs-truckstop-for-a-new-dispatcher/) before selecting a service provider.

## Managing Cash Flow and Factoring

Booking high-paying loads is only half the battle; getting paid quickly keeps your truck on the road. Many carriers use freight factoring companies to receive immediate cash advances on delivered loads rather than waiting 30 to 45 days for broker payouts. When evaluating your operating costs, remember that your dispatcher's fee is separate from your factoring discount rate. If you want to understand how advance rates, reserve accounts, and factoring fees interact with your weekly revenue, explore the details on [Freight Factoring, Recourse vs Non-Recourse](/factoring/freight-factoring-recourse-vs-non-recourse/) to protect your business against non-payment risks.

## Legal and Operational Boundaries

Independent dispatchers operate under specific regulatory frameworks established by the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA). A legitimate dispatcher represents the motor carrier, not the shipper or broker, and cannot legally broker freight unless they also hold a valid broker license and surety bond. When you partner with a dispatcher, you sign a carrier-dispatcher agreement that outlines the scope of work, fee schedules, and payment terms. Always verify that your dispatcher understands federal regulations, including compliance standards detailed in guidelines for [Hours of Service for Dispatchers](/compliance/hours-of-service-for-dispatchers/), so they never book a load that forces your driver to violate legal driving limits.

## Pros and Cons

**Pros**
- Clear percentage or flat-fee pricing models let you accurately forecast overhead costs.
- Percentage structures align the dispatcher's earnings directly with your gross revenue success.
- Frees up owner-operators to focus entirely on driving and maintaining equipment.
- Flexible contract terms allow you to adjust service levels as your fleet grows.

**Best results when**
- You clearly outline all expected administrative tasks in your signed dispatcher agreement.
- You run consistent lanes that allow the dispatcher to negotiate strong rates.
- You maintain open communication about your scheduling preferences and maintenance downtime.
- You regularly review your financial returns to ensure the dispatch fee matches the value generated.

## Who It Is Ideal For

This pricing structure and service model are ideal for independent owner-operators and small fleet owners who want professional load negotiation and back-office support without taking on the overhead of a full-time W-2 employee. It suits operators who prefer performance-based partnerships where the dispatcher is directly motivated to find profitable freight to keep revenue flowing every week.
