## The short answer

You can start dispatching trucks in the United States without a CDL or prior dispatcher experience. The job is arranging freight for a carrier you are contracted with: finding loads, checking the broker, agreeing a rate, and keeping the truck moving. You do need a written working arrangement, a load board, and a clear line between dispatching and brokerage.

## What a truck dispatcher actually does

A truck dispatcher’s job is to keep a carrier’s trucks productive. In practice that means searching for freight that fits the equipment, the driver’s hours, and the lane the truck is already on; checking that the broker or shipper is someone the carrier is willing to haul for; agreeing a rate; confirming pickup and delivery appointments; and staying with the load until the paperwork is in.

The work is mostly phone, email, and a load board, not a steering wheel. You will still think like a driver. A beautiful rate that requires a pickup the driver cannot legally make, or a delivery that parks the truck in a dead lane, is not a good load. The dispatcher who lasts is the one who books freight the truck can actually run.

You may also handle pieces of the back office: sending the rate confirmation to the driver, collecting the bill of lading and proof of delivery, preparing an invoice, or submitting a packet to a factoring company. Those tasks are not optional extras on many independent-dispatcher agreements. Spell them out before you start.

## Dispatcher or broker

This is the line new dispatchers need to understand early. A dispatcher works for, or as an agent of, a motor carrier, booking freight that carrier will haul under its own authority. A freight broker arranges transportation for compensation and needs [FMCSA broker authority](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/registration/broker-registration), including the financial security FMCSA currently requires.

FMCSA has published [guidance on the definitions of broker and bona fide agent](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/06/16/2023-13080/definitions-of-broker-and-bona-fide-agents). In short, a bona fide agent typically has a written continuing agreement with a carrier, is not an intermediary holding out to the public as a broker, and is not in the business of allocating freight among multiple carriers the way a broker is. If your model is “I find a load, then I shop it to whichever carrier will take it,” you are in broker territory.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. If you are unsure which side of the line you are on, read those FMCSA pages and speak with someone who works on motor-carrier compliance. Getting the structure wrong is more expensive than taking a slower path in.

## Skills you can learn without a prior job

Experience helps, but the core skills are learnable before anyone hands you a truck.

**Load boards.** You need to search by origin, destination, equipment, and pickup date, post a truck when that is useful, and read the rate or market tools your plan includes. A new dispatcher should get comfortable with both major boards; start with our comparison of [DAT vs Truckstop for a new dispatcher](/load-boards/dat-vs-truckstop-for-a-new-dispatcher/).

**Broker and credit checks.** Before you commit a truck, you look up the broker or shipper. Load boards and factoring companies offer credit tools for this. A high-paying load from an unknown broker is not a win if the invoice never gets paid.

**Hours of service and equipment.** You do not need to be a driver, but you do need to know what a dry van, a flatbed, a reefer, or a hotshot can take, and how much driving time a driver still has. Booking a load that the driver cannot legally complete is how trust with a carrier ends.

**Geography and deadhead.** The money is in the week, not only in one rate per mile. A strong outbound load that leaves the truck with no freight home can erase the week. Learn the lanes your trucks actually run.

**Paperwork and communication.** Rate confirmations, bills of lading, proof of delivery, invoices, and calm, written updates to the driver and the broker are the job. If you cannot keep a clean file, you cannot dispatch for long.

## A practical path in

There is no single federal license titled “truck dispatcher.” That does not mean you should hang out a shingle on day one and collect trucks. A practical path looks like this.

**Learn the tools first.** Open official learning materials from the load boards, practice searches, and learn how to read a rate confirmation. Follow a week of freight news on a lane you know, even if you are only watching.

**Work under someone else if you can.** A carrier’s operations desk, a small fleet office, or an established dispatch service will teach you the rhythm of check calls, fallback loads, and bad appointments faster than a course will. Paid experience also gives you a reference when you later ask an owner-operator to trust you with the truck.

**Write the carrier agreement before you book.** If you go independent, put in writing which trucks you dispatch, who negotiates rates, who talks to the broker, how you are paid, what happens on a cancelled or unpaid load, and who owns the broker relationship. Do not start on a handshake.

**Start with a small set of trucks.** One or two owner-operators in equipment you understand is enough. Adding trucks before you have a repeatable process is how loads get missed.

**Stay on the dispatcher side of the line.** Book for the carriers you are contracted with. If you want to operate as a broker, register as one and meet FMCSA’s requirements before you hold yourself out that way.

## What you need on day one

The office setup is simple, and it should stay reliable.

You need a computer, a smartphone, a quiet place to take calls, a stable internet connection, and a professional email address. You need a load-board subscription that matches the freight you will actually search. You need a way to sign, store, and send PDFs. You need access to the carrier’s authority documents, insurance certificate, W-9, and any factoring instructions, because brokers will ask for them.

A spreadsheet can track the first truck. When you are booking several trucks, a transportation management system is designed to keep the rate confirmation, the driver assignment, the documents, and the invoice on one record so you are not retyping the same load into four tools.

## How dispatchers get paid

Independent dispatchers are commonly paid a percentage of the hauled load, a weekly or monthly flat fee, or a combination. Staff dispatchers are typically employees or contractors of the carrier or the dispatch service, with a wage or salary instead of a per-load split.

There is no official tariff for dispatch fees. What you can charge depends on the services you actually perform — booking only, or booking plus check calls, invoicing, and paperwork — and on what the carrier already does in-house. Put the fee, the payment timing, and the exceptions in the agreement. A percentage of a load that never pays, or of a load the carrier rejects, should not be an after-the-fact argument.

If a program later offers a referral payment for sending a carrier to a factoring company, a fuel card, or a software tool, treat that as a separate relationship. Disclose it to the carrier if it could affect your advice, and do not let a referral steer a truck toward a worse load or a worse contract.
