## The short answer

Freight factoring turns a delivered load's invoice into cash before the broker's payment terms come due. Under a recourse agreement, you remain responsible if the broker does not pay. Non-recourse factoring is designed to shift certain credit risks to the factor, but the contract still decides what a chargeback can cover. Read those rules before you sign.

## How freight factoring works

Standard broker payment terms are often net 30, and the check can arrive later than the term printed on the rate confirmation. Fuel, insurance, driver pay, and truck payments do not wait. Freight factoring is designed to close that gap.

In a typical sequence, the carrier hauls and delivers the load, then sends the invoice and the required documents — usually the rate confirmation, bill of lading, and proof of delivery — to the factoring company. The factor purchases that receivable, advances a large share of the invoice, and takes on the work of collecting from the broker. A fee, sometimes called a discount or factor rate, is the cost of that advance. Some agreements also hold a small reserve until the broker pays, then release it, minus any adjustments.

Approval is usually about the credit of the broker or shipper, not only the carrier’s own credit file. That is why factoring can be available to a new authority that would not yet qualify for a conventional line of credit. It is also why the factor will want to run broker credit checks before you haul, and why a load from an unknown broker should be checked before it is booked. Load-board tools can help with that first look; see how [DAT and Truckstop handle credit and factorability data](/load-boards/dat-vs-truckstop-for-a-new-dispatcher/).

Factoring is not free money, and it is not a substitute for booking profitable freight. A weak rate with a factoring fee on top is still a weak rate.

## Recourse factoring

Recourse is the simpler risk story, and it is usually the less expensive fee structure.

If the broker pays, the factor is made whole and you have already received your advance. If the broker does not pay within the period set in the contract, the factor can charge the invoice back to you. You may have to return the advance, or the factor may deduct it from later reserves or later invoices. In that model, you have received faster cash, but you have not transferred the credit risk.

Recourse can still be a reasonable fit when the carrier hauls mostly for known, prompt brokers, keeps enough cash to absorb a rare unpaid invoice, and wants a lower fee. It is a poor fit when one unpaid invoice would stop the truck. A new authority with thin reserves should not treat a low recourse fee as a bargain without asking what happens on a single non-payment.

## Non-recourse factoring

Non-recourse factoring is designed to shift certain credit risks to the factor. In the version most people think they are buying, if an approved broker becomes insolvent or otherwise fails as a credit, the factor keeps the loss instead of sending a chargeback.

The important word is “certain.” Non-recourse is a contract term, not a magic label. Many agreements cover a defined event — often bankruptcy or insolvency inside a stated window — and leave other failures with the carrier. A broker that simply stops answering the phone may not meet the definition of a covered default. A payment that arrives late, but eventually arrives, may not be covered either.

Some companies sell a broader form of protection on approved invoices. QP Trucking, for example, offers recourse, non-recourse, and a Pure Non-Recourse option designed so qualifying loads are not charged back. Summar Financial offers Summar Shield, designed to protect approved invoices when a broker fails to pay for reasons beyond the carrier’s responsibility, including delays that run past a short window.

## The exceptions that decide real risk

Most chargebacks are not dramatic bankruptcies. They come from the load itself.

If the consignee claims shortage or damage, if the driver missed an appointment, if the proof of delivery is missing or unclear, or if the invoice does not match the rate confirmation, many factors — including some that advertise non-recourse — will charge the invoice back. Fraud, double brokering, and invoices that were never eligible under the schedule are typically on the carrier, too.

This is why paperwork is not a back-office afterthought. A dispatcher who sends a clean packet — legible documents, the right reference numbers, and a complete delivery record — is doing credit-risk work, not only clerical work. A sloppy packet can turn a “protected” invoice into a recourse event.

Read the exceptions until you can explain them in one paragraph. If you cannot, the label on the program is not doing its job.

## What to read before you sign

Before a carrier signs, get the fee schedule, the advance rate, any reserve, any monthly minimum, the contract term and termination notice, and the full chargeback and dispute list. Ask whether you can choose invoices or must factor all of them. Ask how fuel advances, if offered, are repaid. Ask how long funding takes after a complete packet arrives, and what “complete” means.

Confirm how broker credit checks work, and whether a load hauled for a broker the factor later declines is still your problem. Confirm who talks to the broker about payment — you, or the factor — so your office is not making collection calls the factor has already assigned to itself.

This is general information, not financial or legal advice. A short conversation with an accountant or a transportation attorney is cheaper than an unnoticed clause.


## How to compare two quotes

Put the two offers on one page: advance, fee, reserve, minimums, contract length, and the chargeback list. A lower fee with full recourse can cost more than a slightly higher non-recourse fee the first time a broker fails. The opposite is also true: paying extra for “non-recourse” that only covers a narrow bankruptcy window may not be buying what you think it is.

Ask both companies the same questions about an approved broker that stops paying, a short pay, a cargo claim, and a missing proof of delivery. Write down the answers. The better quote is the one you can explain to the person who signs the agreement, not the one with the more confident sales call.

## Where dispatchers fit

If you are [starting out as a dispatcher](/getting-started/how-to-become-a-truck-dispatcher-with-no-experience/), you may be the person who submits the packet and tracks the advance, but you are rarely the person who should pick a factor on a handshake. The carrier signs, the carrier pays the fee, and the carrier absorbs a chargeback.

Your job is to check the broker before the truck is committed, keep the file complete, and make sure the carrier understands the difference between a lower recourse fee and a non-recourse clause that still has exceptions. If you refer a carrier to a factoring company and may earn a commission for that referral, say so. A labeled affiliate relationship does not change the carrier’s need to read the contract.
