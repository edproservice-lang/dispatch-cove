## The short answer

A truck dispatcher in the United States books freight for a carrier. The driver’s hours of service decide whether that freight can actually be run. Before you accept a rate confirmation, you need a realistic clock: remaining driving time, the 14-hour window, the 30-minute break, the weekly cycle, and enough time left to reach a legal place to stop. A load that only works if everything goes perfectly is not a well-booked load.

This is general information about how those limits affect dispatching. It is not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for the current Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration rules.

## The limits that decide a load

FMCSA’s public summary of the hours-of-service regulations for property-carrying drivers is the place to start. In that summary, the core limits are:

- An 11-hour driving limit, after 10 consecutive hours off duty.
- A 14-hour window: the driver may not drive beyond the 14th consecutive hour after coming on duty, following 10 consecutive hours off duty. Off-duty time does not extend that 14-hour period.
- A 30-minute interruption after 8 cumulative hours of driving without at least a 30-minute break. The summary states that the break may be satisfied by any non-driving period of 30 consecutive minutes.
- A 60- or 70-hour on-duty limit in 7 or 8 consecutive days, with a 34-hour restart available after 34 or more consecutive hours off duty.

Read those figures on [FMCSA’s summary page](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/regulations/hours-service/summary-hours-service-regulations). The page itself notes when it was last updated. If the agency revises a limit, the official summary controls, not this guide.

The same summary also describes a sleeper-berth provision, an adverse driving conditions exception, and a short-haul exception. Those are real parts of the rules. They are also easy to misuse as padding when you are trying to make a tight appointment fit. Treat them as exceptions with conditions, not as extra hours you can spend at the point of booking.

## What a dispatcher has to count

The number on a load board is a rate and a distance, not a clock. You still have to build a clock.

Deadhead is driving. Empty miles to the pickup, between stops, or to a legal parking place consume the 11-hour driving limit and run down the 14-hour window. If you want the pay viewed against those miles, use the [true rate per mile calculator](/tools/true-rate-per-mile/). For hours of service, the same empty miles are time.

On-duty time at a shipper or receiver counts against the 14-hour window even when the wheels are not turning. A late loading appointment, a long queue at the dock, or a lumper wait can erase the time you thought you had for the next leg. Detention is an invoice problem later. It is a hours problem while it is happening.

Appointments are only useful if they sit inside the window the driver actually has. A 6:00 a.m. pickup after a long previous day, or a tight delivery that assumes no traffic and no dock delay, is how loads get booked and then fail. Ask for the driver’s remaining drive time, remaining 14-hour time, and next required break before you promise a broker a window.

The weekly 60- or 70-hour limit ends trips even when a single day still looks open. A driver who is close to the cycle cap cannot legally keep stacking days just because each appointment fits on its own.

## A check before you book

Use a short, repeatable check on every load, not only on the ones that look tight:

1. Confirm the equipment, the weight, and the stops on the posted load, then confirm them again on the rate confirmation.
2. Ask the driver, or read the current log, for remaining drive hours, remaining time in the 14-hour window, and when the next 30-minute break is due.
3. Add a realistic running time for the deadhead and the loaded miles, then add time for fuel, the dock, and a legal place to park after delivery or before the next pickup.
4. Check the 60- or 70-hour cycle, not only today.
5. If the load only works with no delay, or only works by using an exception, do not book it as if the exception were already approved.

This is the same discipline described in [how to become a truck dispatcher with no experience](/getting-started/how-to-become-a-truck-dispatcher-with-no-experience/): a rate the truck cannot legally run is not a good load. The [load-board comparison](/load-boards/dat-vs-truckstop-for-a-new-dispatcher/) will help you find freight. It will not tell you whether the driver can take it.

## Exceptions you should not plan around

FMCSA’s summary includes several provisions that change how time is counted in defined situations: the split sleeper-berth rule, the adverse driving conditions exception, the short-haul exception, and related guidance on personal conveyance. Each of those has conditions. None of them is a planning tool for making an otherwise illegal trip look legal at the moment you call a broker.

If a driver or a carrier uses an exception, they should do it under the current rule and their own compliance process. A dispatcher who books to the edge of an exception is betting the load on a condition that may not apply. Leave a margin instead.

Electronic logging devices record the clock the carrier will be asked to explain. Upcoming coverage of ELD choices for a new authority belongs on the [owner-operator shelf](/owner-operators/). The rule itself still starts with the official summary.
